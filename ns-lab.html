A peek inside a swirling cylinder

Imagine cutting a cylinder of moving fluid down the middle. You’re looking at that slice. The fluid wraps around the dashed center line, so each ring appears on both sides: the left and right patches belong to the same ring.

In the default view, orange and blue mean opposite spin directions. Brightness shows how much spin the fluid carries. The arrows show which way fluid moves within the slice. Think of them as little wind vanes: they turn in place, and their length does not measure speed. Much of the spinning motion goes around the cylinder, out of the screen.

Driven vortices keeps adding spin in two moving regions. Decaying rings lets the motion gradually fade. The other two presets are reference examples: Burgers should stay steady, while Lamb–Oseen slowly spreads out.

Tap or drag in the driven experiment to add spin. The circles mark where your push is being applied. They fade over 0.5 units of simulation time, which can take many real seconds. While paused, your pushes wait for Play or Step.

  • Field switch between spin, twisting strength and flow lines
  • Tap / drag stir; hold Shift to reverse the spin
  • Space pause or play
  • Step advance one small simulation step
  • R reset the experiment
  • V cycle through views

Start with the preview grid for quicker motion. Watch the time counter: simulation time advances more slowly on finer grids. Changing the experiment, viscosity, grid or drive settings starts a fresh run. Contrast only changes how it looks.

The math, if you’re curious

“Axisymmetric” means the flow looks the same as you turn around the center line. We calculate a slice and use that symmetry to describe the whole cylinder.

a = uθ / r   ·   χ = ωθ / r
∂ta + u·∇a = 2a∂zφ + νL₅a + Sa
∂tχ + u·∇χ = ∂z(a²) + νL₅χ
−L₅φ = χ
L₅ = ∂rr + (3/r)∂r + ∂zz

u is velocity, ω is vorticity (local twisting), ν is viscosity, and Sa adds spin in the driven experiment. The helper φ gives the flow within the slice:

ur = −r∂zφ
uz = 2φ + r∂rφ

The ring presets use stationary walls where fluid velocity is zero. Streamlines follow contours of ψ = r²φ. Color limits stay fixed during a run, making changes in strength easier to compare.

What the checks tell us

The reference examples compare the calculation with known solutions. Numerics shows how well each step satisfies the solver’s targets; Charts tracks motion and energy over time. Passing one target does not guarantee that every detail is resolved, especially on a coarse grid.

This lab explores a symmetric fluid model. It does not reproduce the OpenAI paper’s full construction or test its singularity result.
